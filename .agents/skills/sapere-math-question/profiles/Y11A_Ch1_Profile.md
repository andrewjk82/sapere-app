# Chapter Profile: Year 11 Advanced Chapter 1 — Algebra review

**Source:** User-provided *CambridgeMATHS NSW Stage 6 – Mathematics Advanced*, Year 11, 2025 edition, Chapter 1 (sections 1A–1E, printed pp. 2–21).

## Core concepts and section map

- **1A Expanding brackets:** collect like terms; use the distributive law; recognise special expansions such as \((a+b)^2\), \((a-b)^2\), and \((a+b)(a-b)\).
- **1B Factoring:** reverse expansion. Take out a highest common factor first, then use difference of squares, quadratic factoring, or grouping. Recognise perfect-square trinomials.
- **1C Algebraic fractions:** factor completely before cancelling; preserve restrictions from the original denominator. For addition/subtraction use a common denominator; for division multiply by the reciprocal; simplify compound fractions by factoring or multiplying top and bottom by a common denominator.
- **1D Solving quadratic equations:** solve by factoring, completing the square, or the quadratic formula. Choose a method that fits the expression and check candidate roots in the original equation when transformations may introduce restrictions.
- **1E Solving simultaneous equations:** use substitution or elimination to find the ordered pair satisfying both equations; substitute back to verify.

## Standard solution method

1. Identify the operation or equation type and note any excluded values (especially denominators equal to zero).
2. Rewrite using a suitable structure: expand brackets, factor fully, find a common denominator, or arrange a quadratic as \(ax^2+bx+c=0\).
3. Show each algebraic operation on every relevant term. For simultaneous equations, state which equation is substituted or which multiples are added/subtracted.
4. Check the result in the original expression/equations and state the final simplified expression or solution set.

## Common student pitfalls

- Combining unlike terms, e.g. treating \(x\) and \(x^2\) as like terms.
- Forgetting to distribute a factor to every term inside brackets or mishandling a negative sign.
- Cancelling terms across addition. Only common factors may be cancelled, after factoring.
- Losing domain restrictions when cancelling a factor in an algebraic fraction.
- Using the wrong sign when completing the square or substituting into the quadratic formula.
- Solving only one of the two simultaneous equations and not checking the ordered pair in both.
