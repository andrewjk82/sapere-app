# Chapter Profile: Year 11 Advanced Chapter 2 — Numbers and surds

**Source:** User-provided *CambridgeMATHS NSW Stage 6 – Mathematics Advanced*, Year 11, 2025 edition, Chapter 2 (sections 2A–2D, printed pp. 25–39).

## Core concepts and section map

- **2A Real numbers and intervals:** distinguish natural/whole/integer/rational/irrational/real numbers; represent real values on a number line; convert between inequalities and interval notation. Round brackets exclude an endpoint; square brackets include it; infinity always uses a round bracket.
- **2B Surds and their arithmetic:** a surd is an irrational root written exactly. Simplify by taking square factors out of the radicand, then collect like surds; multiply and divide using root laws when defined.
- **2C Further simplification of surds:** simplify expressions involving sums, products, quotients, and conjugate pairs while preserving exact form.
- **2D Rationalising the denominator:** multiply numerator and denominator by the required surd or by the conjugate so the denominator becomes rational; simplify the resulting exact expression.

## Standard solution method

1. Identify whether the task is classifying a number, describing an interval, simplifying a surd, or rationalising a denominator.
2. For intervals, determine whether each boundary value is included and translate that choice consistently between a number line, inequality, and interval notation.
3. For surds, factor the radicand into perfect-square and remaining factors; extract square factors and combine only matching surds.
4. For a denominator containing a sum or difference of surds, multiply top and bottom by its conjugate, use \((a-b)(a+b)=a^2-b^2\), then simplify and check the denominator is rational.
5. Give exact surd form unless the question explicitly requests a decimal approximation.

## Common student pitfalls

- Treating a rational decimal as irrational or assuming every non-integer is irrational.
- Including an excluded endpoint or using a square bracket at infinity.
- Adding radicands, e.g. \(\sqrt a+\sqrt b\ne\sqrt{a+b}\).
- Combining unlike surds before simplifying them.
- Rationalising only the denominator without multiplying the numerator by the same factor.
- Forgetting that multiplying by a conjugate changes the numerator too, or simplifying an exact answer into an unnecessary decimal.
