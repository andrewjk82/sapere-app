# Chapter Profile: Year 11 Advanced Chapter 4 — Equations and inequations

**Source:** User-provided *CambridgeMATHS NSW Stage 6 – Mathematics Advanced*, Year 11, 2025 edition, Chapter 4 (sections 4A–4D, printed pp. 102–119).

## Core concepts and section map

- **4A Linear equations and inequations:** use equivalent operations to isolate the unknown. For inequations, reverse the inequality sign only when multiplying or dividing by a negative number.
- **4B Quadratic equations and inequations:** solve quadratic equations by factoring, completing the square, or the quadratic formula. Solve quadratic inequalities by locating roots and checking the sign of the quadratic between/around the roots (using a sketch or test points).
- **4C The discriminant:** for \(ax^2+bx+c=0\), \(\Delta=b^2-4ac\). If \(\Delta>0\), there are two distinct real roots; if \(\Delta=0\), one repeated real root; if \(\Delta<0\), no real roots. Use these cases to determine parameter values and graph intersections.
- **4D Quadratic identities:** distinguish identities (true for every allowed variable value) from equations (true only for particular solutions). Use expansion, factorisation, substitution, and coefficient comparison to establish identities or determine unknown coefficients.

## Standard solution method

1. Put the equation or inequality into a clear standard form and record any restrictions on the variable.
2. Select and name the method: inverse operations for a linear equation; factorisation/completing the square/quadratic formula for a quadratic equation; roots plus sign analysis for a quadratic inequality; discriminant for root-count questions.
3. Show each operation and preserve inequality direction. For sign analysis, mark roots on a number line and test one value in each interval (or use the parabola’s opening and intercepts).
4. Check candidate solutions in the original equation/inequality and express the final solution set with correct interval endpoints.

## Common student pitfalls

- Reversing an inequality sign during addition/subtraction, or failing to reverse it when dividing by a negative.
- Dividing by an expression that could be zero without checking that case.
- Giving only one root of a quadratic or omitting the repeated-root case.
- Assuming \(\Delta<0\) means the quadratic is always negative; it means there are no real x-intercepts, while the sign also depends on the leading coefficient.
- Solving a quadratic inequality as if it were an equation and not testing intervals.
- Treating an identity as having only the roots of one rearranged equation, or comparing coefficients before both expressions are expanded consistently.
